## 1. Speaker Architecture - Joe's (AMD) Pressure Pyramid (6 stages)

Joe is the dominant speaker. He does NOT present slides - he pressures. His talk is structured as a 6-stage pressure escalation. Each stage has fixed formulas. This is the architecture of a high-pressure supply meeting.

### Stage 1: Customer Demand Framing (Problem Sourcing)

Joe never says "we want X." He says "customers are forcing us to do X." This transfers the blame.

| Speech formula | Original text | Function |
|:---|:---|:---|
| `we are not doing this work because we want to do this work` | "We are not doing this work because we want to do this work. We do not want to do this work is the actions of yourselves and some of your competitors that is forcing us to now go do this work." | Blame transfer - "you forced us" |
| `our customers are telling us to` | "We are now putting huge resources on Turin because our customers are telling us to." | Customer as authority - "we just follow" |
| `the demand is only going to go higher from there` | "the demand is only going to go higher from there" | Demand escalation framing |

**Audrey's lesson**: In English supply meetings, the buyer never says "I want." He says "my customers are forcing me." This removes the buyer's agency and makes the request sound like market reality, not negotiation. When you push a supplier, frame your request as customer-driven: "our customers are telling us to."

### Stage 2: Competitor Threat (Share Loss Warning)

Joe introduces a nameless competitor who is "going all in" and will take SK Hynix's share.

| Speech formula | Original text | Function |
|:---|:---|:---|
| `there's someone else out there that is pricing the MRDIM much lower` | "there's someone else out there that isn't SSSK hynix that is pricing the MRDIM much lower" | Nameless competitor threat |
| `they're going to take all your market share in China` | "And they're going to take all your market share in China." | Direct share-loss prediction |
| `you will have no sales in China` | "Before you know it, you will have no sales in China." | Worst-case escalation |
| `24 months from now, your market share in China will be very, very low` | "24 months from now, your market share in China will be very, very low if you continue these actions" | Timeline + consequence |

**Audrey's lesson**: "There's someone else out there that is X" - never name the competitor, just imply. This creates fear without verifiable claims. And "they're going to take all your market share" - "all" is the weapon. Not "some", "all". Exaggeration is a negotiation tool.

### Stage 3: Timeline Ultimatum (Time Pressure)

Joe compresses the decision window aggressively.

| Speech formula | Original text | Function |
|:---|:---|:---|
| `can you give me an answer on my request before I leave Korea?` | "Check 48 hours from now. Can you give me an answer on my request before I leave Korea?" | Hard deadline - personal departure as lever |
| `I expect us to be having conversations and then executing on them in less than four weeks` | "I expect us to be having conversations and then executing on them in less than four weeks." | Execution speed expectation |
| `we're executing on them in like 40 weeks` (complaint) | "We have conversations. We're executing on them in like 40 weeks. And we still actually haven't started executing." | Speed gap framing |

**Audrey's lesson**: "Before I leave Korea" is a masterful deadline - it ties the answer to a personal event, not a calendar. It is hard to refuse without insulting the visitor. When you need a fast answer, anchor the deadline to a personal stake: "before I leave", "before my review", "before the quarter closes".

### Stage 4: Emotional Escalation (Frustration Display)

Joe uses emotion as a weapon. This is rare in Korean business culture but common in US supply meetings.

| Speech formula | Original text | Function |
|:---|:---|:---|
| `my blood is boiling over` | "my blood is boiling over because this lack of good 128 gigabyte solution is taking Turin and making it less competitive" | Emotional escalation - "blood boiling" |
| `I'm going to be very angry` | "I'm going to be very angry." | Direct emotion threat |
| `it's like talking to blue in the face` | "And it's like talking to blue in the face." | Idiom - "talking to a wall" |
| `I beg you` | "I beg you." | Begging - extreme leverage |
| `my frustration is, we keep saying the same thing over and over again` | "My frustration is, we keep saying the same thing, same thing over and over again, and we're not getting anywhere." | Frustration meta-statement |

**Audrey's lesson**: American executives use emotion openly. "My blood is boiling" is acceptable in a US supply meeting. It is NOT acceptable in Korean. When you face this, do not match the emotion - stay calm and use the SK Hynix deflection patterns in Section 2. But when YOU push a US partner, controlled frustration ("my frustration is...") is effective. "My frustration is, we keep saying the same thing" - stating the frustration as a fact, not an attack.

### Stage 5: Resource Reallocation Threat (Leverage)

Joe threatens to remove engineering resources from SK Hynix and give them to other suppliers.

| Speech formula | Original text | Function |
|:---|:---|:---|
| `we will remove resources from SSSK hynix and apply them to other companies` | "We will then remove the resources from SSSK hynix and apply them to the other companies because we're not aligned strategically." | Resource threat |
| `you guys be clear with us and we will remove resources` | "You guys be clear with us and we will remove resources from SSSK hynix and apply them to other companies because we have the same exact problems." | Conditional resource threat |
| `you also are growing a new supplier by your actions` | "You also are growing a new supplier by your actions." | Blame for competitor rise |
| `I am totally committed to supporting SSSK hynix with all of AMD resources. But...` | "I am totally committed to supporting SSSK hynix with all of AMD resources. But if you're telling me you can't match our strategy..." | Commitment + "But" = threat setup |

**Audrey's lesson**: "I am totally committed to X. But if Y, then Z" - this is the commitment-threat structure. The "totally committed" sounds positive, but it is setup for the "But" threat. When a partner says "I am totally committed to you", the next word is usually "But". Listen for the "But".

### Stage 6: Trust Appeal (Partnership Framing)

After the threats, Joe appeals to trust and partnership.

| Speech formula | Original text | Function |
|:---|:---|:---|
| `the word partnership is built on first trust` | "The word partnership is built on first trust." | Trust appeal |
| `what partners do is trust` | "And what partners do is trust. And so build me those MRDims and build them fast." | Trust + command |
| `there's no profit in it for me to lie to you` | "There's no profit in it for me to lie to you." | Honesty claim |
| `we're going to win with the winners. We're not going to win with the losers` | "We're going to win with the winners. We're not going to win with the losers." | Winner-loser framing |

**Audrey's lesson**: After threatening, Joe appeals to trust. This is the "good cop" turn after "bad cop". "Partnership is built on trust" sounds warm but is actually a demand: "trust me and do what I say". When you hear "partnership is built on trust" in a supply meeting, it means "stop questioning me and execute".

## 2. Hedging & Deflection Strategies (SK Hynix Side)

SK Hynix is on the defensive. The SK Hynix speakers use specific patterns to delay, deflect, and avoid commitment. These are the patterns YOU will use when AMD pushes you.

### Strategy 1: "We cannot decide now" (Decision Deferral)

The most common SK Hynix pattern - refuse to decide in the meeting.

| Situation | Original text | Translation |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Joe demands pricing answer | "Okay, I think I understood. We cannot decide right now here." | "알겠습니다. 지금 당장 여기서 결정할 수는 없습니다." |
| Joe asks for timeline | "But guys, can we please get the right people in the room?" - SK Hynix: "We cannot decide right now here." | "지금 당장 결정은 어렵습니다." |

**Pattern formula**: `We cannot decide right now here. But we will take a look at it with a positive way.`

**Audrey's lesson**: "We cannot decide right now here" is the standard Korean deflection. It is honest but weak in US ears. Joe hears this as "you are avoiding." Strengthen it: "We cannot commit in this meeting, but we will escalate this internally within 48 hours." Add the commitment. The SK Hynix version lacks the commitment, which is why Joe gets more frustrated.

### Strategy 2: "Complicated decision" (Complexity Deflection)

Frame the decision as too complex for a quick answer.

| Situation | Original text | Translation |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Joe demands 128GB tall MRDIM | "It's going to be a very complicated decision not only for the just pricing, but related to many things related to even the other products as well." | "가격뿐 아니라 다른 제품까지 관련된 매우 복잡한 결정입니다." |

**Pattern formula**: `It's a very complicated decision, not only for X, but related to Y and Z as well.`

**Audrey's lesson**: "It's a complicated decision" - this is the Korean "검토해 보겠습니다" in English. It works once. But Joe has heard it "for three quarters". When you use "complicated decision" repeatedly, it loses all power. Use it ONCE per topic, then commit to a specific follow-up date. "It's a complicated decision. We will give you a preliminary answer by next Friday."

### Strategy 3: "Positive way" (Hedged Commitment)

SK Hynix says they will "look at it positively" - a non-commitment commitment.

| Situation | Original text | Translation |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Joe pushes for MRDIM | "So we will take a look at it with a positive way." | "긍정적으로 검토하겠습니다." |

**Pattern formula**: `We will take a look at it with a positive way.`

**Audrey's lesson**: "With a positive way" is Korean-English. A US speaker would say "We will review this favorably" or "We will look at this with priority." The phrase "with a positive way" is grammatically off - "in a positive way" or "positively" is correct. But more importantly, "positive way" is a NON-commitment. Joe hears "you will not do it." When you want to hedge, say "We will prioritize this review" - it sounds like action but commits to nothing.

### Strategy 4: Absent Decision-Maker Deflection

SK Hynix deflects by saying the marketing/decision people are not in the room.

| Situation | Original text | Translation |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Joe asks for pricing | "Is there anybody from the marketing team here right now?" - SK Hynix: silence. Joe: "So then I'd suggest we don't do this now." | "마케팅팀이 여기 없습니다." |
| SK Hynix deflects | "So we will forward that one." | "해당 내용은 전달하겠습니다." |

**Pattern formula**: `The right people are not in the room. We will forward this to them.`

**Audrey's lesson**: "We will forward that one" is weak. "Forward" means "send an email". Joe wants a decision, not an email. When you deflect to an absent decision-maker, add a timeline: "We will forward this to marketing and get you a response within 48 hours." The timeline is what matters - "forward" alone sounds like "we will forget".

### Strategy 5: "First time hearing" (Surprise Claim)

SK Hynix claims this is the first time they are hearing the request.

| Situation | Original text | Translation |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Joe says 128 tall MRDIM | "We need to go back and check because this is the first time that we hear 128 tall MRDIM on Gen 2." | "128 tall MRDIM Gen2 요청은 처음 듣습니다." |
| SK Hynix challenges Joe's timeline | "Could you please tell us when AMD asked us 128 tall because we've been in our recollection. It's actually the first time that you come to request 128 than to tall MRDIM." | "AMD가 128 tall을 요청한 것이 언제인지 알려주세요. 우리 기억으로는 처음입니다." |

**Pattern formula**: `This is the first time that we hear X. We need to go back and check.`

**Audrey's lesson**: "This is the first time we hear X" is a polite way to say "you never asked before." It is a factual challenge. Joe counters by pulling up the deck from "two quarters ago." When you claim "first time", make sure your own records support it. Joe's counter: "Even in last Qtget, we talked about it. And we can, you guys have the deck." - he proves SK Hynix already has the information. The "first time" claim backfired.

## 3. Polite Challenge Patterns (Joe's Aggressive Challenge - and SK Hynix Counter)

This meeting has NO polite challenge from Joe. Joe is direct, even rude. But there are a few patterns SK Hynix uses to push back politely. Section 4 (negotiation) covers Joe's direct demands.

### Challenge Type 1: First-Time Claim (Factual Counter)

| Speech formula | Original text | Function |
|:---|:---|:---|
| `this is the first time that we hear X` | "This is the first time that we hear 128 tall MRDIM on Gen 2." | Polite factual pushback |
| `we need to go back and check` | "We need to go back and check" | Delay via verification |

**Audrey's lesson**: "We need to go back and check" is the Korean "확인해 보겠습니다" - it is polite and non-committal. It works as a delay, but Joe sees through it. Use it only when you genuinely need to verify.

### Challenge Type 2: Pricing Reality Pushback (SK Hynix)

| Speech formula | Original text | Function |
|:---|:---|:---|
| `I'm a little bit confused from the price perspective` | "I'm a little bit confused from the price perspective. You mean that 128 gigabyte based on the 16 gigabit MRDIM is cheaper than the 128 gigabyte based on the 32 gigabit RDIM?" | Polite challenge via "confused" framing |
| `our price per se is out of the show` | "Our price per se is out of the show." | Deflect pricing blame |
| `we can talk about the price premium with our marketing team` | "We can talk about the price premium with our marketing team." | Defer to marketing |

**Audrey's lesson**: "I'm a little bit confused from the X perspective" is a polite challenge. You say "confused" but you are actually saying "I think you are wrong." This is softer than "I disagree." Use this when you want to challenge without confrontation: "I'm a little bit confused - can you clarify whether X is true?"

### Challenge Type 3: Direct Factual Question (SK Hynix)

| Speech formula | Original text | Function |
|:---|:---|:---|
| `Could you please tell us when AMD asked us 128 tall?` | "Could you please tell us when AMD asked us 128 tall because we've been in our recollection. It's actually the first time that you come to request 128 than to tall MRDIM." | Polite but firm - demands evidence |
| `If you look at the Qtget slide, please share with us. When was it?` | "If you look at the Qtget slide, please share with us. When was it?" | Request for evidence |

**Audrey's lesson**: "Could you please tell us when X" - this is how you politely call someone out. You ask for evidence of their claim. Joe had to pull up the deck. When a partner makes a claim you doubt, ask "could you please share when that was discussed?" - it forces them to prove it or back down.

## 4. Negotiation & Action Item Language (CORE for Type B)

This is the key section for Type B meetings. Joe uses aggressive negotiation language; SK Hynix uses deflection. Both are learning targets.

### Negotiation Language - Joe's Demand Patterns

| Speech formula | Speaker | Original text | Function |
|:---|:---:|:---|:---|
| Volume request | Joe | "we'd love to sample 16 gigabit tall MRDIM for Turin from you guys" | "we'd love to sample X" - sample request |
| Timeline target | Joe | "I expect us to be having conversations and then executing on them in less than four weeks" | "I expect X in less than Y" - execution target |
| Hard deadline | Joe | "can you give me an answer on my request before I leave Korea? 48 hours." | "before I leave" + "48 hours" - deadline |
| Spec pushback | Joe | "you guys are not being aggressive on 128 gigabyte 32 gigabit DRAM pricing" | "you are not being aggressive on X" - pricing pushback |
| Milestone coordination | Joe | "It'll take you three or four weeks to build us the dims. Get them to us and we'll put you guys in the queue" | "Get them to us and we'll put you in the queue" - milestone + lever |
| Aligned with | Joe | "But we've got to be aligned on the tactical. We must be aligned on the strategic." | "aligned on X" - alignment demand |
| Under consideration | SK Hynix | "So we will take a look at it with a positive way" | "take a look with a positive way" - non-committal review |
| Fair share demand | Joe | "I want my fair share. And my fair share should be based on market share at the CSPs, which means give me 65% of your buffers" | "I want my fair share based on X" - allocation formula |
| Threat | Joe | "we will remove the resources from SSSK hynix and apply them to the other companies" | "remove resources from X and apply to Y" - resource threat |
| Conditional commitment | SK Hynix | "the number of samples that AMD need, we will support" | "the number X needs, we will support" - conditional support |

**Audrey's lesson - 5 key negotiation formulas for Type B:**

1. **"we'd love to sample X for Y from you"** - The sample request. "we'd love to" sounds soft but is a demand. Use this when requesting samples: "we'd love to sample 16Gbit tall MRDIM for Turin."

2. **"can you give me an answer before X?"** - The deadline with personal stake. "Before I leave Korea" is stronger than "by Friday". Tie the deadline to an event.

3. **"we must be aligned on X"** - The alignment demand. "Aligned on the tactical" and "aligned on the strategic" - two levels. Use this when you and the partner have different strategies: "we must be aligned on the roadmap direction."

4. **"I want my fair share based on X"** - The allocation formula. Joe defines "fair share" as 65% based on CSP market share. When you negotiate allocation, define the formula, not the number: "fair share should be based on our joint platform volume."

5. **"the number of samples X needs, we will support"** - SK Hynix's commitment. This is the conditional commitment - "we will support the number you need" without giving a specific number. Use this when you want to commit in principle but not in quantity.

### Action Item Language

| Speech formula | Speaker | Original text | Function |
|:---|:---:|:---|:---|
| Action item definition | Joe | "the action item that SSSK hynix needs to take out of this conversation is to review the pricing strategy on 128 with the 32 gigabyte base RDIM" | "the action item X needs to take is Y" - assigns action item |
| Sample commitment | SK Hynix | "the number of samples that AMD need, we will support. Very clear. We'll get those samples ASAP." | "we will support. Very clear. ASAP." - triple commitment |
| Timeline commitment | SK Hynix | "we will hurry up" | "we will hurry up" - speed commitment (weak) |
| Recognition statement | SK Hynix | "We recognize the your request. And then we will hurry up." | "we recognize X. And we will Y." - acknowledgment + action |
| Follow-up promise | Joe | "Check 48 hours from now. Can you give me an answer before I leave Korea?" | "Check 48 hours from now" - follow-up deadline |

**Audrey's lesson**: Joe assigns action items TO SK Hynix: "the action item SK Hynix needs to take is to review pricing." When you are the buyer, you can assign action items to the supplier. When you are the supplier (SK Hynix position), you receive action items. The key is to repeat them back: "To confirm the action item - we will review pricing on 128GB 32Gbit RDIM and respond within 48 hours." Repeating confirms understanding.

## 8. Audrey's Teaching Notes

### Register Analysis
This meeting is **high-pressure supply negotiation** register. Joe (AMD) is the buyer with leverage; SK Hynix is the supplier under pressure. This is the OPPOSITE of the Marvell meeting (where Marvell was the seller pitching to SK Hynix). Key register features:
- **Joe's register**: confrontational, emotional, direct, threatening. Uses "you guys" repeatedly (informal aggressive). Uses "I" statements heavily ("I want", "I expect", "I beg you"). Short sentences for impact.
- **SK Hynix register**: defensive, hedging, deferential, non-committal. Uses "we" statements. Longer sentences with qualifications. Korean-English patterns ("positive way", "out of the show", "don't be my death").

You need to learn BOTH registers because:
1. When AMD pushes YOU, you will hear Joe's patterns - recognize them and prepare responses
2. When YOU push a US partner, you can use controlled versions of Joe's patterns
3. When YOU are pushed, use SK Hynix's deflection patterns but ADD commitments (the SK Hynix version is too weak)

### Pragmatics Core
1. **Blame transfer**: "We are not doing this work because we want to - you forced us." Joe never owns the demand. He transfers it to customers and competitors. This is the #1 pragmatics pattern. When you push a supplier, frame it as "our customers are forcing us" - not "we want."

2. **Emotional escalation as leverage**: "My blood is boiling", "I beg you", "I'm going to be very angry." In US supply meetings, controlled emotion is a tool. It is NOT loss of control - it is deliberate. When you hear this, do NOT match the emotion. Stay calm, acknowledge, commit to a timeline.

3. **The "But" threat structure**: "I am totally committed to X. But if Y, then Z." The "totally committed" is NOT a commitment - it is setup for the threat. Always listen for the "But" after a positive statement in a supply meeting.

4. **Personal deadline**: "Before I leave Korea" is stronger than "by Friday." It ties the deadline to a personal event. The supplier cannot refuse without insulting the visitor. When you need a fast answer, use a personal stake.

5. **Fair share formula**: Joe defines "fair share" as 65% based on CSP market share. He rejects "50-50" as unfair. The lesson: in allocation negotiation, define the FORMULA, not the number. "Fair share based on X" is stronger than "give me 65%."

### Top 5 Must-Use Expressions
1. **"we'd love to sample X for Y"** - Sample request formula. Use when requesting samples.
2. **"can you give me an answer before I leave?"** - Personal deadline. Use when you need a fast answer.
3. **"we cannot decide right now here, but we will escalate within 48 hours"** - Strengthened deflection. Use when you cannot commit in the meeting.
4. **"we must be aligned on the strategic"** - Alignment demand. Use when strategies diverge.
5. **"I want my fair share based on X"** - Allocation formula. Use when negotiating component allocation.

### Korean vs English Comparison
| Korean style | English (this meeting) | Difference |
|:---|:---|:---|
| "검토해 보겠습니다" | "We cannot decide right now here. We will take a look with a positive way." | Korean is shorter; English SK Hynix adds "positive way" but still lacks commitment |
| "확인해 보겠습니다" | "We need to go back and check" | Direct translation - works but Joe sees through it |
| "긍정적으로 검토하겠습니다" | "We will take a look at it with a positive way" | "positive way" is Korean-English; US would say "prioritize this review" |
